# Walking and the public–private interface
### What Rome and the European city actually measure, what the evidence supports, and the rules worth encoding in a village plan generator

Research synthesis, November 2026. Four parallel reviews: Roman and European urban form; the walkability evidence base; the public/private interface; and pedestrian design standards.

**Evidence grades used throughout**

| Tag | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| `CODE` | A legal requirement somewhere, jurisdiction-specific |
| `GUIDANCE` | Published design guidance (NACTO, GSDG, Manual for Streets, CROW) — not law |
| `EVIDENCE` | Empirical study with stated sample or method |
| `DOCTRINE` | Design theory with wide professional acceptance but weak causal evidence |
| `PRECEDENT` | Historic built example, measured — descriptive, not prescriptive |

---

## 0. The short version

1. **The historic city is fine-grained and continuous.** Rome's colonial module was 35.5 m (one *actus*); medieval plots were 6–12 m wide and 12–40 m deep; Bologna has 62 km of continuous porticoes. The grain, not the monument, is what makes these places walkable.
2. **The measured street is narrower than modern standards.** Roman side streets 3.5–6 m, medieval principal streets 6–8 m, Cerdà's Eixample 20 m with a 16–22 m cornice. Enclosure ratios H/W of 0.8–1.8 are the norm, not 0.2–0.3.
3. **The evidence for walking is about networks and destinations, not aesthetics.** Preferred block length 80–120 m; the empirically supported intersection density range is far lower than the widely quoted "15–20 per hectare"; 400 m and 800 m *network* catchments are the tests that matter.
4. **The public→private gradient is doctrine, not a measured law** — but specific devices have real dimensions (2 m front gardens, 1.5–2.1 m visually permeable walls, ≤8 dwellings per core, shared assets tenure-blind) and the evidence does support one thing strongly: **the layout of shared space decides where encounter and conflict happen**, far more than unit-level mixing.
5. **Scale changes the prescription.** A 13-acre village has no meaningful "intersection density" problem — the whole site is inside an 800 m walk. What matters there is internal permeability, the position of the community core on the everyday path, and the design of each lot's threshold.

## 1. The measured historic city

### 1.1 Rome and the colonial grid

**What transfers.** A 35 m module with a 1:1 to 1:4 aspect ratio, ordinary streets of 3.5–6 m and through-streets of 12–15 m, and a hard height ceiling near 20 m. Note the Roman distinction that keeps being reinvented: the *cardo* was often not much wider than other streets — it was differently paved and porticoed. Hierarchy was expressed by surface and edge, not only by width.

**The piazzas are rooms, and the proportions prove it.** Navona is 1:3.7 but only because it is a former stadium track; San Marco's 175 × 82 m is ~2:1; Campo is nearly circular; Vitruvius asks for 3:2. A public space that is 3.5:1 is a widened street, not a room. This matters for our plaza (see §6).

**The portico is the single most transferable device in this study.** A 2.5–3 m deep, 3 m high covered walkway, continuous along both sides of a street, does four things at once: it is public circulation, it is the private frontage's threshold, it is weather protection, and it is the commercial edge. Bologna made it a building obligation in 1288 — arguably the first floor-area bonus for arcades.

## 2. What actually makes people walk

### 2.1 Network, not shape

| Metric | Empirically supported value | Caveat |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Intersection density | IPEN Adult study (11,615 adults, 14 cities): **~98 intersections/km²** associated with 80% probability of any transport walking; **122/km²** with sufficient walking; reported ranges **98–334/km²** and **122–356/km²** | `EVIDENCE`, cross-sectional, not causal |
| The widely quoted "15–20 intersections/ha" | = 1,500–2,000/km², **far above** the IPEN ranges | Not supported as a universal target — do not cite it |
| "60 intersections/sq mi" | ≈ 23/km² | Not comparable to the above; counting rules differ |
| Block length | **80–120 m** preferred; UN-Habitat illustrates 111 m; Queensland requires a pedestrian link above 130 m, 250 m absolute ceiling | `GUIDANCE` converging with `PRECEDENT` |
| Route directness | network ÷ straight-line, closer to 1 is better; **≤1.3** proposed as a local design rule for routine trips | No consensus threshold — state it as a local choice |

**Space syntax as a diagnostic, not a score.** A meta-analysis found positive associations between pedestrian movement and **integration** (effect size 0.206, 95% CI 0.173–0.238) and **choice** (0.481, 0.391–0.561); connectivity's pooled estimate was not significant. Angular measures outperformed topological ones. Separately, perceived destination availability accounted for **42%** of integration's association with walking — configuration and destinations work together.

**Practical reading:** a fine road grid is not automatically a permeable *walking* network. Count pedestrian links, crossings and barriers separately from vehicle intersections.

### 2.2 Distance and destination

- 400 m ≈ 5 minutes, 800 m ≈ 10 minutes at 4.8 km/h. A 15-minute walk is about 1.2 km one way.
- Melbourne (2,349 adults): destinations within **401–800 m** were associated with increased odds of walking; the association was **not** found at 801–1,200 m.
- Destination-intensity comparison: OR **1.49** at 400 m, **1.71** at 800 m, **1.86** at 1,200 m.
- *Manual for Streets*: facilities within about 10 minutes / 800 m.

**Use network distance, not circles, and test 400 m and 800 m separately.** Both the "15-minute city" and the "20-minute neighbourhood" are policy framings, not proven thresholds.

### 2.3 Street-level comfort

The strongest evidence is for a **continuous usable route**, destinations, and safety. Everything else is weaker than design culture assumes:

- **Enclosure:** studies do not consistently find more pedestrians on more enclosed streets. A street-life study of 881 block faces found no positive enclosure effect once density and land use were considered.
- **Greenery/shade:** a systematic review found no significant walking association for tree enclosure, though shade is a real heat-comfort requirement.
- **Seating:** evidence that street furniture, especially seating, increases pedestrian activity — but no robust universal effect size.
- **Traffic:** speed and volume drive danger and perceived comfort; crossing availability and delay matter more than intersection count.

### 2.4 Cul-de-sacs and superblocks

- **Against cul-de-sacs for walking:** they make routes indirect and lengthen trips to services; UK guidance criticises them explicitly.
- **For cul-de-sacs, but not for walking:** UK crime research reports lower burglary on true cul-de-sacs (through roads 93% more crime, leaky cul-de-sacs 110% more). That is a crime result, not a walkability result.
- **The resolution:** separate **vehicle permeability from pedestrian permeability**. A short quiet vehicle court is fine if a continuous, legible footpath connects its end to the surrounding network.
- **Barcelona superblocks:** at Sant Antoni, NO₂ fell 25% and PM10 17%; residents reported better tranquillity and sleep. Observed walking was 18.2% lower after a year than in the *first week* — a comparison with no pre-intervention baseline, so it does not show the intervention reduced walking. Superblocks are traffic-space interventions, not substitutes for a connected network.

## 3. The public–private gradient

### 3.1 The doctrine

The four-step gradient — public → semi-public → semi-private → private — converges from four literatures that mostly did not cite each other:

- **Chermayeff & Alexander (1963):** an "anatomy of privacy" as a hierarchy of spaces linked while retaining autonomy. The critique of it is that it is purely spatial, with no account of meaning or use.
- **Gehl:** movement "from the more private to the gradually more public", and the **soft edge** — "if the edge of a public space works, so does the space". Practice rules from Gehl Architects: **10 doorways per 100 m of façade**, **4 m ground-floor height**.
- **Alexander:** *Degree of Publicness (36)*, *Circulation Realms (98)*, *Family of Entrances (102)*, *Entrance Transition (112)*, *Hierarchy of Open Space (114)*, *Intimacy Gradient (127)*, *Common Areas at the Heart (129)*, *Private Terrace on the Street (140)*. Each realm entered through a marked gateway, getting smaller inward.
- **Bentley et al.:** permeability is both physical and visual; entrances at the interface "enrich public space by increasing the level of activity around its edges"; privacy itself is graded, so only the most private activities need screening.
- **Hertzberger:** the threshold as a designed object — "creating a setting for welcomes and farewells". His bench under shelter at De Overloop belongs to house and street at once.

### 3.2 What the evidence supports

**Defensible space is weaker than its influence.** Newman's four principles (territoriality, natural surveillance, image, milieu) came from access geometry — slabs with one entrance and long corridors are hard to survey. But:

- An official review of Newman's own datasets found "only limited statistically reliable conclusions" because defensible space was never operationally defined; **building height** and **welfare share** both had significant effects.
- Chenoweth's before/after study in two developments (n=209) found that creating defensible space **did not** produce territorial behaviour or social fabric.
- Hillier: "a bad book about a very important subject". Taylor (2002): social, cultural and economic factors explain more than design; some studies find no effect.

**Two doctrines point opposite ways.** McKenzie & McKenzie compress Newman into "maximise private and semi-private, avoid semi-public where possible". Gehl and Alexander want rich semi-public edges. That disagreement is unresolved and should be stated as such in any design code.

**What the evidence does support, consistently:** the **layout of shared space** governs where encounter and conflict happen. Roberts' three UK case studies found "the organization of the housing units mattered less for social interaction than did the organization of public space". Irish and UK mixed-tenure research points to community centres and shared facilities as potentially more productive of interaction than the street. A multiple-case study of London high-density schemes found most encounters in circulation and entrance areas — making their quality a major social benefit. And Tersteeg & Pinkster show the failure mode: mixed tenure with **segregated use of facilities** and conflicting expectations of shared space (quiet balconies vs. children's play in the common yard) reinforces distance rather than dissolving it.

### 3.3 Devices, with the dimensions that exist

| Device | Dimension / rule | Grade |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Front garden / setback | Victorian London terraces **~2 m** | `PRECEDENT` |
| Front wall / fence | Victoria max **1.5 m** within 3 m of a street (**2 m** in a Transport Zone); NSW solid walls max **2.1 m** with **≥1.5 m** setback and **≥50%** visually permeable infill | `CODE` (local) |
| Light court | fully open to sky, min dimension **⅓ of perimeter wall height**, min area **4 m²** | `CODE` (NSW) — a daylight rule, not a social one |
| Shared entrance / core | **≤8 dwellings per floor per core**, corridors **≥1500 mm**, communal space overlooked and available to all tenures | `CODE` (London) |
| Shared surface (woonerf) | legal **15 km/h**; widened areas **≥4.5 m wide, ≤20 m long, ≥40 m apart**; speed-reducing elements **≤50 m** apart | `CODE` (NL) / `GUIDANCE` (CROW) |
| UK traffic calming | elements **≤30 m** apart; no part of a precinct **>300–400 m** from a normal road | `GUIDANCE` |
| Arcade / portico | **2.5–3 m** clear depth (min), **3 m** clear height; 5–6 m for ceremonial routes | `PRECEDENT` (Bologna) |
| Gate + communal court (Gemeindebau) | gateway filters the city, **stair** is the recognition unit; Karl-Marx-Hof 1,272 flats / 156,000 m², 1.1 km long; Sandleitenhof 1,531 flats | `PRECEDENT` |
| Danish co-housing | ring of low buildings around a common space; common house sized for one person reading mail as well as a crowd | `PRECEDENT` + `DOCTRINE` |
| Terrace / mews | public front, private back, small front garden; mews door directly onto a shared pedestrian surface | `PRECEDENT` |

Two cautions on Vienna: the courtyard-as-community reading is partly retrospective — a current survey of Karl-Marx-Hof and Sandleiten found "vacant communal spaces and signage stating: no cycling, no football playing". And stair-based access, not the courtyard, is where recognition actually happens.

### 3.4 Rules for shared facilities that the evidence does support

1. Put shared facilities **on the everyday path** between parking, units and the common building, not in a leftover corner.
2. Give access areas **"slack"** — space beyond circulation — because encounters cluster there.
3. **One neutral hub** between groups, and **equal-status mixing** (50/50) to avoid stigma.
4. Make common rooms slightly **larger** than the minimum — Danish survey data suggests small common rooms are used less.
5. Make shared outdoor space **tenure-blind and overlooked**, reached through the entrances.

## 4. Synthesis: rules for a walkable village

### 4.1 Scale check before copying anything

A 13-acre parcel (5.3 ha, ~230 × 195 m) sits entirely inside a 400 m walk from its centre. Copying city-scale metrics here would be cargo cult: **intersection density is not the binding constraint**, and neither is the 15-minute city.

What *is* binding at village scale:

- **Internal permeability** — can you walk from any lot to the community core without leaving the site or walking along a driveway edge?
- **Core adjacency** — is the clubhouse/pool/garden/parking on the way between the entrance and the lots, or tucked behind them?
- **Threshold quality** — each lot needs a semi-private zone, or the pad is simply a parking space with a house behind it.
- **Block grain** — 100 m is the right order here too, because that is one comfortable walk and it produces the loops and cross-links that make a village legible.

### 4.2 The rule set

| # | Rule | Target | Provenance |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Block / cluster length | **80–120 m** (262–394 ft) between pedestrian links | `GUIDANCE` + `PRECEDENT` |
| 2 | Residential lane width | **6–8 m** (20–26 ft) shared surface; 4.5 m clear minimum path | `PRECEDENT` + CROW |
| 3 | Design speed on shared lanes | **10–15 km/h**, self-enforced by geometry | `GUIDANCE` |
| 4 | Pedestrian link spacing | Every **80–100 m**; a link through every block over 100 m | `GUIDANCE` |
| 5 | Lot frontage | **6–12 m**; a door per lot → ≥10 doors per 100 m | `PRECEDENT` + Gehl practice |
| 6 | Semi-private threshold | **2 m** deep strip: stoop, porch, low wall or planting | `PRECEDENT` |
| 7 | Boundary height / permeability | **1.2–1.5 m**, ≥50% visually open; sightlines to doors kept clear | `CODE` (local) |
| 8 | Plaza proportion | **1:1.5 to 1:2**, never shallower than 1:2.5 | `PRECEDENT` (Vitruvius, San Marco) |
| 9 | Arcade, if modelled | **3 m** deep, **3 m** clear, continuous both sides | `PRECEDENT` (Bologna) |
| 10 | Parking position | Visitor/resident bays at the **edge or rear**, never across a primary frontage; a maximum ratio, not a minimum | `GUIDANCE` |
| 11 | Shared facilities | On the highest-integration route between entry and lots; **10 m** walk from most lots | `EVIDENCE` (layout of public space) |
| 12 | Dwelling cluster size | **6–10** per shared entrance/court | `CODE` (London, 8/core) + `PRECEDENT` |
| 13 | Courtyard / island clear dimension | ≥ **8 m** clear, and ≥ ½ the enclosing height for daylight | `CODE` (daylight logic) |
| 14 | Tree canopy over paths | **2 m** clear stem; shade over seating | `GUIDANCE` |
| 15 | Emergency access | **3.7 m** clear to within **45 m** of every building; turning head at dead ends >20 m | `CODE` (UK) |

### 4.3 A walkability scorecard

Report these, with values labelled as local rules rather than proven thresholds:

1. **Median block length** and the 90th percentile; count blocks over 120 m.
2. **Pedestrian link density** and **dead-end share**, separately from the vehicle network.
3. **Core catchment:** % of lots within **400 m network distance** of the community core (target 100% at village scale), and within 800 m.
4. **Route directness** from the furthest lot to the core; investigate above 1.5.
5. **Frontage grain:** doors per 100 m of lane frontage.
6. **Threshold provision:** % of lots with a ≥2 m semi-private strip.
7. **Parking on frontage:** % of stalls visible from a primary pedestrian route (target: low).
8. **Enclosure:** median H/W along lanes; warn below 0.3 or above 2.0.
9. **Comfort:** continuous walkable surface, seating count, shade, crossings and their delay.

## 6. Where it falls short — and the specific changes implied

1. **There is no pedestrian network.** Lots front pavement directly; `bands` has a 0 ft verge, so walking happens in the drive. *Add:* a 1.5 m (5 ft) footway on at least one side of every lane, or declare lanes shared surfaces and enforce 15 km/h by geometry.
2. **No through-block pedestrian links.** Grid blocks get a green with no path; loop islands get trees but no crossing. *Add:* a 3 m path across every block over 100 m, connected to the lanes at both ends.
3. **The plaza is 3.5:1.** `plazaW = clamp(w × 0.24, 78, 170)`, `plazaH = clamp(h × 0.26, 26, 48)` gives ~170 × 48 ft = **52 × 15 m, 3.5:1** — a widened drive, not a room. *Change:* `plazaH ≥ plazaW / 2` (Vitruvius 3:2, San Marco 2:1).
4. **No threshold.** The pad meets the lane; there is no stoop, porch, front garden or gate. *Add:* a **2 m** semi-private strip per lot, with a low (1.2–1.5 m) visually permeable boundary, and keep the door visible from the lane.
5. **Every lot parks on its frontage.** `site.stall` sits at the lot's front corner. *Change:* move the stall behind the dwelling or into a shared court; report "% stalls on frontage".
6. **No crossings or link spacing logic.** `bands` reserves two 20 ft gaps per lane for pedestrian crossings; the other three patterns have none. *Add:* crossings every 80–100 m, and make the gaps coincide with block paths.
7. **No courtyard minimum.** Loop islands can degenerate to a shrub strip. *Add:* reject islands under 8 m clear and merge them into the adjacent green.
8. **No metrics.** The schedule reports lots, stalls and beds but nothing about walking. *Add:* the §4.3 scorecard.

## 7. Contested, unverified, and what not to claim

- **"15–20 intersections per hectare"** has no support as a universal walking target; it is 4–15× the empirical IPEN ranges.
- **"Bologna has 38 km of porticoes"** is not in the primary source; UNESCO's figure is **62 km total, 42 km inside the walls**.
- **The Servian Wall is early 4th century BC**, not 5th.
- **Ostia's castrum** has two conflicting measurements (194 × 125 m vs 182 × 155 m).
- **Timgad's street widths** are contested (4–5 m vs ~14.8 m); the *cardo* may not have been wider, only harder-paved.
- **Cul-de-sac crime findings** are about burglary, not walking; do not cite them as walkability evidence.
- **Superblock walking effects** have not been demonstrated against a proper baseline.
- **Defensible space** does not have the causal evidence its influence implies; Hillier's critique stands.
- **Gehl's 10 doors/100 m and 4 m ground floor** are practice guidance, not measured thresholds.
- **ITE's 2010 walkable-thoroughfare guidance was rescinded in November 2025** — do not cite it as current.
- **6–8 m residential street widths** could not be verified as a current ITE/CNU standard; treat as context-dependent.
